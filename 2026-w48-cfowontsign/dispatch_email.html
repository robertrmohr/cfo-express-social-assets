When the CFO Won't Sign

A budget your finance chief will not sign is not a budget. It is a wish.

This month, a finance chief walked out rather than sign the numbers. At the same time, two of America's biggest companies handed the CEO job to their CFOs, and a third CFO was named in a shareholder lawsuit. The finance seat is front-page news. The question for every owner is simple: would your finance lead sign your numbers, and what happens if they won't?

What happened

  • On Nov 13, Chicago's chief financial officer, Ashlee Gabrysch, resigned after about three months on the job. The resignation letter said the move came rather than "compromise my integrity." Bloomberg reports the CFO had raised concerns about the city's budget practices, including inconsistent deficit calculations. It is the city's third CFO change this year, and the exit raised fears about the city's credit rating.
  • The Chicago City Council voted 45–5 for a review of the city's finances. The city comptroller is serving as acting CFO.
  • Home Depot announced that its CFO, Richard McPhail, becomes CEO on Nov 23. At Nvidia, CFO Colette Kress has been interim CEO since Nov 12, while still serving as CFO, and led the company's Nov 18 earnings call.
  • Intel shareholders filed a proposed class action naming the CEO and CFO David Zinsner, alleging they did not properly disclose the impact of weakening PC demand.
  • Minutes released Nov 18 show nearly all Fed officials expect to raise rates by a quarter point at the Dec 8–9 meeting. Governor Christopher Waller has argued for a half point. The Fed's target range is 3.75–4.00%.

Why it hits your P&L and balance sheet

Chicago is a city, not a company. The lesson still carries straight into a private business. When the person closest to the numbers will not stand behind them, four things on your books are at risk.

  • Your numbers. If "deficit," "cash" or "EBITDA" means something different depending on who calculates it, every decision built on it is soft. Lenders and buyers find these gaps fast.
  • Your bench. CFOs are being promoted, poached and pushed out. If your finance lead left on Friday, who would close the month, and who would talk to the bank?
  • Your liability. The Intel suit is a reminder that what you tell investors and lenders has to match what you know. Owners sign loan certificates, covenant reports and tax returns too.
  • Your cost of capital. Credit-rating fears followed Chicago's CFO out the door. A lender reacts the same way to a surprise change in your finance team, and it will do so in a month when the Fed is likely to raise rates.

I have seen owners walk into a bank meeting with a cash number that changed depending on which report they pulled. Nothing was hidden. Nobody had written down how the number was built. The bank noticed anyway. The fix is not a new system. It is a finance leader who is expected to say "I will not sign that" and is thanked for it.

5 moves to make this week

  1. Write down the definitions. For each number you report to a lender, board or buyer (cash, EBITDA, backlog, covenant ratios), record how it is calculated, from which source, and by whom.
  2. Reconcile the versions. Put the management, lender and tax versions of your key numbers side by side. Every difference gets a written explanation.
  3. Ask your finance lead one direct question: "Is there any number in our 2027 budget you would not sign?" Make it safe to answer yes.
  4. Name a backup for the finance seat. Document who closes the month, who has bank and payroll access, and where the close checklist lives. Then test it: could someone else close November?
  5. Re-run 2027 debt service at today's rate plus 25 and 50 basis points before you sign the budget. List which loans reprice first after Dec 9: lines of credit, variable-rate term debt, then anything due for renewal in 2027.

Questions management must answer

  • Who signs our numbers, and what would make them refuse?
  • Do we calculate cash and EBITDA the same way every month, in writing?
  • If our finance lead left tomorrow, who covers the seat for the next 90 days?
  • Which of our loans reprice first if the Fed hikes on Dec 9?

If your finance lead refused to sign your numbers tomorrow, would you already know why?

What to watch next week

  • The Fed. Speeches in the run-up to the Dec 8–9 meeting will show whether a quarter point or a half point is in play.
  • Tariff refunds. Several public companies have earmarked refund money for bonuses, and seven senators have asked the FTC to look at refunds that were not passed on to customers. If you are getting a refund, decide now how you will book it and what you will tell customers.
  • Chicago's financial review. It will show how a budget gap grows when the definitions drift.

Next week: year-end cash. What to collect, what to defer and what to lock in before the Dec 9 rate decision.
